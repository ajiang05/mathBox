\title[Group rings and Pr\"ufer]{When is the ring of integer-valued polynomials over a group ring a Pr\"ufer domain?}

\begin{abstract}
In their study of the ring of integer-valued polynomials in noncommutative algebra, Peruginelli and Werner characterized the algebras for which this ring is a Pr\"ufer domain. Here, we apply their results to the case of group algebras.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In all the paper, \recordmath{D} denotes a commutative integral domain distinct from its quotient field \recordmath{K}.

\begin{definition}[only for this paper]
A \recordmath{D}-algebra \recordmath{\Aa} is said to be a {\em nice \recordmath{D}-algebra} if it is unitary, is finitely generated and torsion-free as a \recordmath{D}-module and is such that \recordmath{\Aa\cap K=D}.
\end{definition}

In the sequel, \recordmath{\Aa} always denotes a nice \recordmath{D}-algebra. Let \recordmath{\Bb=K\otimes_D\Aa} and \recordmath{m=\dim_K \Bb}. With these hypotheses, we may assume that \recordmath{\Bb} contains \recordmath{K} and \recordmath{\Aa}.

\begin{examples} The following rings are nice \recordmath{D}-algebras.

a) \recordmath{\Mm_n(D)} the ring of square matrices of size \recordmath{n} with entries in \recordmath{D}.

b) \recordmath{\SH_D} the ring of Hamilton quaternions with coefficients in \recordmath{D}.

c) \recordmath{D[G]} the group ring where \recordmath{G} is a finite group.	
\end{examples}

In a recent paper, Peruginelli and Werner characterized the case where the following ring
 of {\em integer-valued polynomials on} \recordmath{\Aa} is a Pr\"ufer domain:
 \[\recorddisplay{\int_K(\Aa)=\{f(X)\in K[X]\mid f(\Aa)\subseteq\Aa\}.}\]

\begin{theorem}\cite[Theorem 1.7\,(1)] {bib:PW2026}\label{th:B}
Assume that \recordmath{\Aa} is a nice \recordmath{D}-algebra. Then, the ring \recordmath{\int_K(\Aa)} is a Pr\"ufer domain if and only if \recordmath{D} itself is a Pr\"ufer domain and \recordmath{\Aa=\Aa'} where \recordmath{\Aa'} is the set formed by the elements of \recordmath{\Bb} which are integral over \recordmath{D}.
\end{theorem}

Applying their results to the first two examples of nice algebras, they show that a matrix algebra \recordmath{\Mm_n(D)} where \recordmath{n\geq 2} never leads to a Pr\"ufer domain (\cite[\S\,4]{bib:LW2012} or \cite[Corollary 3.4]{bib:PW2016}), whereas the ring \recordmath{\int_{\SQ}(\SH_{\SZ_{(2)}})} is a Pr\"ufer domain where \recordmath{\SH_{\SZ_{(2)}}} denotes the Hurwitz quaternion ring on \recordmath{\SZ_{(2)}} \cite[Theorem 5.4]{bib:PW2026}, that is,
\[\recorddisplay{\SH_{\SZ_{(2)}}=\left\{a_0+a_1{\bf i}+a_2{\bf j}+a_3{\bf k}\mid a_i\in \SZ_{(2)}\;\forall i \textrm{ or } a_i\in \SZ_{(2)}+\frac{1}{2}\;\forall i\right\}.}\]

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

When the Jacobson radical \recordmath{J(D)} of \recordmath{D} is equal to \recordmath{(0)} and \recordmath{\int_K(\Aa)} is a Pr\"ufer domain, then the \recordmath{D}-algebra \recordmath{\Aa} is necessarily commutative and we have a more precise result:

\begin{theorem}\cite[Theorem 1.7\,(2)]{bib:PW2026}\label{th:14}
Assume that that the Jacobson radical \recordmath{J(D)} of \recordmath{D} is \recordmath{(0)} and that \recordmath{\int(D)} is a Pr\"ufer domain. Then, \recordmath{\int_K(\Aa)} is a Pr\"ufer domain if and only if 

\centerline{\recordmath{\Aa\simeq \prod_{i=1}^t \Aa_i}} 

\smallskip

\noindent where \recordmath{\Aa_i} is the integral closure of \recordmath{D} in a finite extension \recordmath{F_i} of \recordmath{K}. In this case, each \recordmath{\Aa_i} is a commutative integral domain such that \recordmath{\int_{F_i}(\Aa_i)} is a Pr\"ufer domain.
\end{theorem}

\section{Group rings}

Let \recordmath{G} be a group and let \recordmath{D[G]} be the \recordmath{D}-algebra  
\[\recorddisplay{D[G]=\{\sum_{g\in G}a_g\,g\;\big\vert \; a_g\in D \textrm{  almost all zero} \}.}\] 
It is a free \recordmath{D}-module with basis \recordmath{\{g\}_{g\in G}} and a multiplication defined by \[\recorddisplay{\left(\sum_{h\in G}a_hh\right)\times\left(\sum_{k\in G}b_kk\right)=\sum_{g\in G}\left(\sum_{hk=g}a_hb_k\right)g.}\]
The group ring  \recordmath{D[G]} is a nice \recordmath{D}-algebra if and only if \recordmath{G} is a finite group. Thus, from now on we assume that \recordmath{G} is a finite group. Of course, if \recordmath{\Aa=D[G]}, then \recordmath{\Bb=K[G]} and 

\centerline{\recordmath{m=\dim_K K[G]=\mathrm{rk}_D (D[G])=\vert G\vert}.}

\smallskip

\noindent By Theorem~\ref{th:14}, if \recordmath{J(D)=(0)} and if \recordmath{\int_K(D[G])} is a Pr\"ufer domain, then \recordmath{D[G]} is commutative. Clearly, \recordmath{D[G]} is commutative if and only if \recordmath{G} is abelian.

\medskip

The purpose of this text is to characterise the finite groups \recordmath{G} such that 
\[\recorddisplay{\int_{K}(\Oo_K[G])=\{f\in K[X]\mid f(\Oo_K[G])\subseteq \Oo_K[G]\}}\]
is a Pr\"ufer domain, where \recordmath{K} denotes an algebraic number field and \recordmath{\Oo_K} its ring of integers. As previously said, \recordmath{G} has to be abelian. We are going to prove that this necessary condition is also sufficient.

\begin{example}
Let \recordmath{C_p} be a cyclic group whose order is a prime number \recordmath{p} and let \recordmath{\zeta_p\in\SC} be a \recordmath{p}-th primitive root of unity. We have the following ring isomorphisms:
\[\recorddisplay{\SZ[C_p]\simeq \SZ[X]/(X^p-1)\simeq\SZ[X]/(X-1)\times\SZ[X]/(1+X+\ldots+X^{p-1})\simeq\SZ\times\SZ[\zeta_m].}\]
Consequently, 
\[\recorddisplay{\int_{\SQ}(\SZ[C_p])=\int_{\SQ}(\SZ)\cap\int_{\SQ}(\SZ[\zeta_m])=\int_{\SQ}(\SZ[\zeta_m]).}\]
But we know that this last ring is a Pr\"ufer domain (cf. \cite{bib:LW2012} or, for instance, see \cite[Corollary VIII.60]{bib:C2}).
\end{example}

In fact, this example may be easily generalized thanks to the following general result that we recall:
  
\begin{theorem}\cite[Theorem 1]{bib:PW1950}\label{thm:2.6}
If \recordmath{G} is a finite abelian group of order \recordmath{n} and if the characteristic of the field \recordmath{K} does not divides \recordmath{n}, the group algebra \recordmath{K[G]} is iomorphic to a product of rings of the form
\[\recorddisplay{K[G]\simeq \prod_{d\vert n}(K(\zeta_d))^{a_d}}\] 
where \recordmath{a_d\in\SN}  and \recordmath{\zeta_d} is a \recordmath{d}-th primitive root of unity. More precisely, 
\[\recorddisplay{a_d=\frac{\#\{\,g\in G \mid \textrm{ord}(g)=d\,\}}{[\,K(\zeta_d)\,:\,K\,]}.}\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}
Assume that \recordmath{K} is an algebraic number field, with ring of integers \recordmath{\Oo_K}, and that \recordmath{G} is a finite group with exponent \recordmath{m}. 
The ring \recordmath{\int_K(\Oo_K[G])} is a Pr\"ufer domain if and only if \recordmath{G} is commutative. In that case,  \[\recorddisplay{\int_{K}(\Oo_K[G])=\int_{K}(\Oo_K[\zeta_m]).}\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{th:14}, if \recordmath{\int_K(\Oo_K[G])} is a Pr\"ufer domain, then \recordmath{G} has to be commutative. Conversely, assume that \recordmath{G} is commutative, then it follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:2.6} that, \recordmath{\Oo_K[G]=\prod_{d\vert m}(\Oo_K[\zeta_d])^{a_d}}. Consequently:
\[\recorddisplay{\int_{K}(\Oo_K[G])=\cap_{d\vert m}\int_{K}(\Oo_K[\zeta_d])=\int_{K}(\Oo_K[\zeta_m])=\int_K(\Oo_{K(\zeta_m)}).}\]	
Finally, (by \cite[Theorem 3.7]{bib:LW2012} or \cite[Corollary VIII.60]{bib:C2}), \recordmath{\int_K(\Oo_K[G])} is a Pr\"ufer domain.
\end{proof}

\section{Annexe}

Un module \recordmath{M} est dit {\em simple} s'il n'est pas r\'eduit \`a \recordmath{\{0\}} et ne  contient pas d'autre sous-module que \recordmath{\{0\}} et lui-m\^eme.

\smallskip

Si \recordmath{A} est un anneau et \recordmath{\mm} un id\'eal (\`a gauche) de \recordmath{A}, le \recordmath{A}-module \recordmath{A/\mm} est simple si et seulement si \recordmath{\mm} est un id\'eal (\`a gauche) maximal.

\smallskip

Un module est dit {\em semi-simple} s'il v\'erifie les conditions \'equivalentes suivantes :

\noindent (i) \recordmath{M} est somme de sous-modules simples,

\noindent (ii) \recordmath{M} est somme directe de sous-modules simples,

\noindent (iii) tout sous-module de \recordmath{M} est facteur direct.

\smallskip

Un anneau \recordmath{A} est dit {\em semi-simple} si en tant que module sur lui-m\^eme il est semi-simple.

\smallskip

Si l'anneau \recordmath{A} est semi-simple, tout \recordmath{A}-module est semi-simple.

\smallskip

\noindent{\bf Wikipedia}. {\em Un anneau est semi-simple si et seulement s'il est artinien et semi-primitif.}

\smallskip

Un anneau semi-simple \recordmath{A} est dit {\em simple} s'il n'est pas r\'eduit \`a \recordmath{\{0\}} et s'il v\'erifie les conditions \'equivalentes suivantes :

\noindent (i) il ne contient q'une classe de \recordmath{A}-module simple,

\noindent (ii) \recordmath{A} et \recordmath{\{0\}} sont les seuls id\'eaux bilat\`eres de \recordmath{A}.

\smallskip

\noindent{\bf Thm}. {\em Un anneau simple est isomorphe \`a un anneau de matrices \recordmath{\Mm_n(D)} o\`u \recordmath{D} est un corps \recordmath{(}commutatif ou non\recordmath{)}.}
